# Make a persuasive case

Build the strongest honest case for the user's requested position or action. Keep the actual audience, medium, and stakes in view. A persuasive paragraph, board proposal, academic argument, and landing page need different amounts of context and evidence.

## Identify the decision

Determine what the reader should do or believe, their likely current position, and what they need to be convinced. Identify the requested next step and the user's real offer, evidence, or authority. Infer routine details from context; ask only when a missing fact would change the substance of the case.

Separate the user's desired conclusion from the support currently available. Research factual claims when needed and tools allow it. With only supplied material or no research tools, stay within that record and disclose material gaps. Do not claim to have verified something you could not inspect.

## Choose the reason that matters

Lead with the point most relevant to this reader's decision. Connect the proposal to a concrete outcome, then explain why the evidence supports it. Features become reasons only when their relevance to the audience is clear. Use specificity drawn from the record, not invented numbers, testimonials, deadlines, relationships, or capabilities.

Address the strongest relevant objection in terms a reasonable opponent would recognize. Explain whether the evidence answers it, narrows the claim, or supports a smaller commitment. For a short advertisement, a relevant limitation may need only a precise qualifier. For substantive advocacy, develop the reasoning and counterargument. Do not force an objection paragraph into every format.

Use confidence proportionate to the support. Distinguish measured outcomes from projections and a proposed benefit from an established result. If contrary evidence defeats the requested claim, say so and offer the strongest defensible alternative. Do not hide a material tradeoff to make the prose sound decisive.

Let the next step fit the reader's readiness and the evidence. A bounded pilot, conversation, purchase, or policy choice should be requested plainly. Add urgency only when a real timing constraint supports it. Preserve the user's exact commitments and supplied proof-of-work links.

## Examples

Inflated: “This transformative program will revolutionize access.”

Grounded: “Approve a six-week pilot for $1,200. It will let us measure demand before committing to a full program.”

Overclaim: “A one-day test recorded eighteen loans, proving lasting demand.”

Defensible: “The one-day test recorded eighteen loans. A pilot would show whether demand continues.”

## Revise for persuasion

Make one targeted revision at the weakest link: an unclear ask, an irrelevant benefit, unsupported certainty, or an unanswered objection. Check the factual basis and whether the reader can see both the requested decision and the reason to make it. Keep required citations and genre conventions. Return the persuasive text, without an internal strategy report unless requested. Drafting does not authorize sending, publishing, or making commitments on the user's behalf.
