# Write a message someone can answer

Draft a complete message suited to the recipient and the relationship. Keep the user's intent, real history, and commitments intact. A request to draft correspondence does not authorize sending it, scheduling a meeting, or making an external commitment.

## Understand the exchange

Identify the recipient, what they already know, the reason for writing now, and the response the user wants. Check the prior exchange or supplied project history when available and relevant. Ask only if a missing fact materially changes the message; otherwise draft with the known context and use a clear placeholder only for an essential missing item.

Use what the user actually did. Do not invent familiarity, admiration, a referral, an attachment, availability, a deadline, or a promise from the recipient. Preserve provided names, dates, proof-of-work links, and descriptions of completed work. Verify additional facts only when needed and tools allow it. If earlier messages or sources cannot be accessed, do not imply that you read them.

## Make the message easy to place and answer

Give enough context for this recipient to recognize who is writing and why. Put the principal ask early enough to find, with the detail needed to answer it. A stranger may need a short introduction; a reply in an active exchange may need none. Avoid retelling a project history the recipient already knows.

Match the register to the relationship and stakes. Be warm without manufactured intimacy, respectful without excessive deference, and direct without making the request sound like an obligation. A concise acknowledgement or thanks should refer to something real. Routine email need not sound like a formal application.

Keep one main ask, or clearly separate a small number of related questions. Make the requested effort and next step concrete: feedback on one issue, a short conversation, a specific document, or an introduction. Include dates and time zones when needed and known. Do not create urgency merely to increase the chance of a reply.

Use a specific subject line for a new email when useful. Follow the existing thread's format for a reply. Include a greeting and sign-off appropriate to the medium; a short text message need not imitate a letter. Preserve supplied copy exactly when the user requests exact wording.

## Examples

Generic: “I am reaching out to explore synergies and would love to connect.”

Concrete: “Could I ask for 15 minutes of advice on the prototype? The two-minute demo is here: [supplied link].”

Overfamiliar: “As you know, I have always admired your pioneering work.”

Grounded: “We spoke briefly after class. I have since built the prototype we discussed.” Use the second sentence only if that history is supplied.

## Read it as the recipient

Make one targeted revision for a buried ask, unnecessary context, awkward tone, or unsupported assertion about the relationship. Check whether the recipient can understand the request and answer it with reasonable effort. Return the ready-to-use draft, with alternatives or commentary only when requested or needed to flag an essential gap.
