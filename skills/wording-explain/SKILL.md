# Explain clearly

Help the reader form an accurate, usable understanding. Keep the user's requested scope, format, and level of detail. This skill improves the prose within the task; it does not replace a requested document, code change, or source-review workflow.

## Establish the reader's question

Identify what the reader wants to understand, what they already know, and what misunderstanding would matter. Infer ordinary context from the request. Ask only when missing information would materially change the explanation; otherwise make a reasonable assumption and proceed.

For a beginner, introduce necessary terms and prerequisites. For a specialist, preserve useful technical distinctions and remove elementary detours. For a factual update, lead with what changed and what it means for that reader.

Use supplied sources as the factual boundary when the user limits the task to them. When new research is needed and tools are available, verify the relevant claims. When tools or sources are unavailable, work with the supplied material and identify the consequential gap. Never imply that a source was read or a fact checked when it was not.

## Build the explanation

Give the direct answer early, then develop the reason, mechanism, example, or qualification needed to understand it. Connect each paragraph to the question that came before it. A definition alone may not explain how something works.

Name the actor, action, object, and consequence where they are known and useful. Replace an abstract label with the actual change it describes. Make pronouns point to an identifiable thing. Explain unfamiliar terms on first use without replacing necessary terms with inaccurate synonyms.

Choose a structure that fits: a short answer, connected explanation, FAQ, comparison, or worked example. Use a list when the items are genuinely parallel. Avoid adding a ceremonial introduction, repeated recap, or identical sections to every explanation.

Keep conditions and causal links intact. Do not turn “may” into “will,” a correlation into a cause, or a limited observation into a general rule. Label hypothetical examples; do not invent measurements, mechanisms, or product features to make the explanation feel concrete. Brevity should reduce reader effort while preserving meaning.

## Examples

Vague: “The system operationalizes seamless synchronization.”

Concrete, if supported: “The app saves your notes on the laptop. Turn on sync to keep a copy on your other devices.”

Incomplete: “Turning off sync stops uploads.”

Complete, if the distinction matters: “Turning off sync stops new uploads. It does not delete copies already on the server.”

## Check the reader's understanding

Make one targeted revision for the largest remaining obstacle: a buried answer, undefined term, missing connection, ambiguous referent, or unnecessary repetition. Check that the reader could explain the main point and its important limit in their own words. Return the finished text; include drafting commentary only when requested or needed to disclose an unresolved factual gap.
